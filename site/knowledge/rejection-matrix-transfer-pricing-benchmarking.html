Benchmarking

How to document a rejection matrix that survives review.

A rejection matrix is the most challenged part of a TP benchmarking file. What it must record, the reason codes that hold up, and the five mistakes that make a search look arbitrary.

Arcubus Advisors18 June 20267 min read

Key takeaways

  • A rejection matrix records every company excluded from the search and the specific reason for each exclusion — not a generic label.
  • Reason codes must map back to the approved search strategy; a reason that appears nowhere in the strategy note reads as a result-driven exclusion.
  • Counts must reconcile: companies in, companies removed per screen, companies accepted. A file that does not add up loses the argument before the reasoning is read.
  • Keep the evidence for each qualitative rejection — the sentence from the annual report or website that justified it.

Almost every challenge to a benchmarking study lands in the same place. Not the arithmetic of the interquartile range, not the choice of profit level indicator — the exclusions. A reviewer asks why a company that looks comparable is not in the final set, and the answer has to be in the file.

That answer is the rejection matrix: a company-by-company record of everything the search threw out and why. Done properly it takes an afternoon. Done badly it costs a year of credibility.

What the matrix must record

A defensible matrix has one row per company that entered the qualitative review, and at least these columns:

  • Company identifier — name and database ID, so the row can be traced back to the extract.
  • Screen that caught it — the specific criterion from the approved strategy, cited the way the strategy words it.
  • Reason — the fact relied on, in a sentence. Not "not comparable".
  • Source — annual report page, website section, or database field the fact came from.
  • Reviewer and date — who made the call, so a second reviewer can re-perform it.

The quantitative screens sit above this as a funnel table: companies in the initial set, then the count removed by each screen in the order it was applied, then the count entering qualitative review. Those two artefacts together are the search log.

Reason codes that hold up

Standardised codes make the matrix readable and force discipline. A workable set:

CodeMeaningEvidence expected
ACTActivity not comparable to the tested transactionBusiness description extract naming the actual activity
RPTRelated-party revenue above the accepted thresholdDatabase field or note to accounts with the percentage
IPOwns or licenses significant non-routine intangiblesBalance-sheet intangibles, or licence income in the notes
DATAFinancial data unavailable for the required yearsExtract showing the missing years
LOSSPersistent losses over the tested windowOperating margin series for the years tested
SIZETurnover outside the accepted bandTurnover figure against the stated band
FUNCFunctional profile materially different (e.g. full-risk vs limited-risk)Description of risks borne, from the annual report

Every code in the matrix must appear in the approved search strategy. If a code shows up that the strategy never mentioned, the file now says the criteria were invented after the results were seen — which is exactly the inference you are trying to avoid.

The five mistakes

1. Generic reasons

"Not functionally comparable" is a conclusion, not a reason. The reason is the fact: manufactures under its own brand and bears finished-goods inventory risk. The conclusion follows from it.

2. Counts that do not reconcile

If the funnel says 412 companies entered qualitative review and the matrix has 380 rows, nothing else in the file will be read charitably. Reconcile before you write.

3. Asymmetric rigour

Rejections are documented in detail; acceptances get a tick. A reviewer reads that as a search designed to reach a range. Record why each accepted company passed each screen, in the same words used to reject others.

4. Evidence that has evaporated

A website that justified an exclusion in April may not exist in November. Save the extract or the screenshot with the file at the moment of the decision.

5. Silent late exclusions

A company dropped after preliminary results — because the margin looked odd — must be documented like any other rejection, with a reason that would have applied before the margin was known. If no such reason exists, it belongs in the set.

How this fits the wider file

The rejection matrix is one of three artefacts that make a benchmarking study reproducible: the approved search strategy note (what we said we would do), the search log and rejection matrix (what we did), and the range workings (what it produced). Any reviewer can then re-perform the study without asking a question.

At Arcubus all three are handed over as annexures with every benchmarking deliverable, on white-label engagements included — the file has to stand up without us in the room.

Questions

On this topic.

Is a rejection matrix mandatory?

No rule names the document, but the OECD Guidelines and most local documentation rules require the search process and selection criteria to be documented and reproducible. A rejection matrix is simply the practical form that requirement takes. In an audit it is usually the first annexure requested.

How much detail is enough per rejection?

Enough that a reader who has never seen the search can agree with the decision without opening the database: the company name, the screen that caught it, the specific fact relied on, and the source of that fact.

Can we reuse last year’s rejection matrix?

You can carry forward the reasoning for companies whose facts have not changed, but each year needs a fresh check: activity descriptions change, related-party revenue moves across thresholds, and loss-making patterns reverse. Carry forward the file, not the conclusion.
