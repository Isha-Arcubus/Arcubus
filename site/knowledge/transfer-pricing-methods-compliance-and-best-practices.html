Transfer Pricing

Transfer Pricing: Methods, Compliance, and Best Practices.

Transfer pricing governs how transactions between related entities within a multinational group are priced. Applying the arm’s-length principle helps businesses establish fair intercompany prices, manage tax risks, and comply with applicable regulations across jurisdictions.

Arcubus Advisors12 October 20266 min read

Key takeaways

    Transfer pricing determines prices for transactions between related companies. The arm’s-length principle is the foundation of transfer pricing compliance. Common methods include CUP, resale price, cost plus, TNMM, and profit split. Accurate documentation helps businesses justify their intercompany pricing. Regular reviews reduce tax disputes, penalties, and compliance risks.

Transfer pricing refers to the prices charged for goods, services, intellectual property, loans, and other transactions between related entities within the same corporate group. For example, when a parent company supplies products to its subsidiary in another country, the price charged for those products is an intercompany transfer price.

These transactions are important because the price assigned to them can affect the taxable profits reported by each entity. Tax authorities therefore examine whether related-party transactions reflect commercially reasonable terms and comply with applicable tax laws.

A well-designed transfer pricing policy helps businesses maintain consistent pricing practices, support their tax positions, and manage cross-border tax obligations.

The arm’s-length principle is the central concept in international transfer pricing. It generally requires transactions between related entities to be priced as though the parties were independent businesses operating under comparable circumstances.

For instance, if a company provides IT support to an overseas group entity, the service fee should be evaluated against the functions performed, assets used, risks assumed, and relevant market conditions.

The OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines provide an important international reference for applying this principle. However, businesses must also consider the domestic legislation and administrative requirements of each country in which they operate.

Selecting an appropriate transfer pricing method depends on the nature of the transaction, the availability of reliable comparable data, and the functions performed by the related entities.

1. Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) Method

This method compares the price charged in a related-party transaction with the price charged in a comparable transaction between independent parties. It is particularly useful when sufficiently comparable transactions are available.

2. Resale Price Method

This method starts with the price at which a product purchased from a related entity is resold to an independent customer. An appropriate gross margin is deducted to determine an arm’s-length purchase price. It can be useful for distribution businesses that do not significantly transform the products they sell.

Questions

On this topic.

Why is transfer pricing important for businesses?

Transfer pricing is important because it affects how profits and expenses are allocated among related entities in different jurisdictions. Appropriate pricing and supporting documentation help businesses comply with tax regulations, reduce the risk of disputes and penalties, and improve transparency in intercompany transactions.

FAQ 2: What happens if a company does not comply with transfer pricing regulations?

Non-compliance may result in tax adjustments, additional tax liabilities, interest, penalties, and prolonged disputes with tax authorities. Businesses may also face increased compliance costs and potential double taxation if different jurisdictions make conflicting adjustments. Maintaining appropriate documentation and reviewing transfer pricing policies regularly can help mitigate these risks.
